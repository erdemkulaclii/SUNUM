## Q1. How is "influence operation" / "coordinated inauthentic behavior" (CIB) defined in the academic and platform literature?

### Takeaway
Two families of definitions exist. (a) Academic ones treat information operations as *participatory, collaborative work* that mixes orchestrated and organic actors (Starbird et al.) or as *political communication* strategies (Freelon & Wells). (b) Platform ones (Meta's "CIB", Gleicher 2018) are *behaviour-based enforcement categories*: deception about identity and coordination, not about content truth. Recent peer-reviewed work (Rogers & Righetti 2025) shows Meta's CIB definition has *narrowed* over time. That matters directly for how the 2022 Doppelgänger list should be read.

## Q2. What do source-cue and credibility studies imply about why impersonating established outlets could work?

### Takeaway
The credibility literature gives a two-sided, testable argument. **Why it could work:** online users rely on heuristics (reputation, endorsement, consistency, "authority" cues), they often do not remember which brand delivered a story, and they rarely read URLs. A cloned site copies exactly the cues these heuristics use (logo, layout, brand name) while only the domain differs. **Limits:** experimental work shows that making publisher cues more prominent barely changes belief in headlines, except when the publisher and the headline are mismatched. Doppelgänger engineers exactly that mismatch in reverse: implausible pro-Kremlin content under a *trusted* brand. So brand impersonation is a strategy to borrow trust, not a guaranteed effect.

### Inferences
- A defensible thesis sentence: *Doppelgänger is a strategy of "borrowed credibility" (ödünç alınmış güvenilirlik). It transfers the reputation heuristic attached to a trusted brand (Metzger et al. 2010; Pennycook & Rand 2019) onto content the brand never produced. It relies on weak brand attribution in distributed environments (Kalogeropoulos et al. 2019) and poor URL literacy (Dhamija et al. 2006; Reynolds et al. 2020).*
- Dias et al. (2020) should be presented as a **counter-argument and caveat**, not as support. Source cues are weak on average, but they matter in *mismatch* conditions. Doppelgänger creates a deliberate mismatch (implausible content, trusted brand), which may either lend credibility or trigger suspicion. Effectiveness is an empirical question that the Meta list cannot answer.
- The 95.8% Germany targeting fits the logic that clones are only valuable where the brand has high *local* reputation capital.

### Gaps
- No experimental study was found that tests *brand-cloned* news sites (as opposed to unknown fake outlets) on belief. This is a real research gap worth stating on a "future research" slide.
- No German-audience brand-trust figures were retrieved here. The Reuters Institute *Digital News Report* (Germany pages) would be the standard grey source.

## Q3. Which security papers measure domain squatting, how do they classify variants, and can their typology be applied to the Meta list?

### Takeaway
The security literature gives a ready-made typology of five squatting types: **typo-, bit-, homograph-, combo- and wrongTLD-squatting** (Tian et al. 2018). A Doppelgänger domain that keeps the *exact* brand label (`spiegel`, `bild`) and swaps the TLD is best classified as **wrongTLD-squatting** (also called TLD-squatting or TLD-swap). It is *not* typosquatting in the strict sense of Szurdi et al. (2014) or Agten et al. (2015), which model character-level errors in the second-level label. Domains that add tokens to the brand (brand + word) are **combosquatting** (Kintis et al. 2017). The new-gTLD measurement literature (Halvorson et al. 2015; Korczyński et al. 2018) explains why .online, .site and .website appear: cheap, loosely policed new gTLDs that brands rarely register defensively.

Needle in a haystack: Tracking down elite phishing domains in the wild. *Proceedings of the Internet Measurement Conference (IMC '18)*, Boston. DOI 10.1145/3278532.3278569. **Supports:** the five-type squatting typology (**typo, bits, homograph, combo, wrongTLD**). DOI 10.1109/ARES.2016.84 (a survey and taxonomy); Moore, T., & Edelman, B. (2010). Measuring the perpetrators and funders of typosquatting. *Financial Cryptography 2010*, LNCS 6052. DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-14577-3_15 (the economics of squatting). — [doi.org/10.1109/ARES.2016.84](https://doi.org/10.1109/ARES.2016.84); [doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-14577-3_15](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-14577-3_15)
- [V, grey] Real Doppelgänger examples from DFRLab (2024): washingtonpost.pm, foxnews.in. Both are exact brand label plus a different TLD, i.e. wrongTLD-squatting in the Tian et al. sense. — [DFRLab](https://dfrlab.org/2024/09/18/doppelganger-us-election/)

### Inferences
- **Concrete method suggestion:** code all 311 Meta domains into the Tian et al. typology using simple rules. (1) First label is *identical* to the genuine outlet's label and the TLD differs → **wrongTLD-squatting**. (2) First label contains the brand plus extra tokens (for example `spiegel-news`) → **combosquatting**. (3) Edit distance 1 from the brand → **typosquatting**. (4) Unicode or look-alike characters → **homograph**. Report the share of each. The user's finding ("same first label reused across many last labels") is then a quantitative *wrongTLD-squatting* result that can be tied to Tian et al. (2018) and Korczyński et al. (2018). This turns a descriptive observation into a measurement placed in the literature.
- The repetition of one brand label across many TLDs is consistent with a *disposable infrastructure / takedown-resilience* logic: when one domain is blocked, the next TLD replaces it. Kintis et al.'s finding on long-lived squats, and Tian et al.'s on blacklist evasion, support the plausibility. This is an inference, not a proven intent.
- Precise wording: Doppelgänger domains are **"brand-exact, TLD-variant look-alike domains"** (marka-birebir, uzantı-değişken benzer alan adları), not "typo domains".

### Gaps
- I did not verify whether any of the 311 Meta domains are combo- or typo-variants. That requires coding the list itself.
- The exact list of typo models in Agten et al. (2015) and Szurdi et al. (2014) is given from knowledge, not re-read this session. Check the papers before quoting specific category names.

## Q4. Key works on Russian information operations, strategic narratives, reflexive control and the "firehose of falsehood"

### Takeaway
Use **strategic narrative** (Miskimmon, O'Loughlin & Roselle) as the meta-theory of *why* states communicate: to shape the meaning of international order. Use **reflexive control** (Thomas 2004) and the **"firehose of falsehood"** (Paul & Matthews 2016) as models of Russian *method*. Use **active measures / forgery** history (Kragh & Åsberg 2017; Rid 2020) to place news cloning in a long Soviet tradition of forged documents and fake publications. Computational work (Golovchenko et al.; Hanley & Durumeric) shows how state-media narratives spread across platforms.

## Q5. Platform takedown transparency and the epistemics of platform-released datasets

### Takeaway
Takedown data such as Meta's 2022 Doppelgänger indicator list is **an enforcement record, not a sample of the operation**. It is shaped by (a) the platform's shifting definitions (Rogers & Righetti 2025), (b) what the platform could detect and chose to disclose (transparency critiques: Ananny & Crawford 2018; Suzor et al. 2019; Urman & Makhortykh 2023), and (c) the post-API research environment, in which researchers depend on platform-curated data (Freelon 2018; Bruns 2019). Empirical work shows that many inauthentic accounts survive takedowns (Im et al. 2020; a 2025 TikTok CIB preprint), so takedown sets are *censored from below*. The GDELT finding (two days of mostly US and English-language coverage) adds a second layer: media attention to takedowns is itself selective.

## Q6 (added by scope update). Hybrid warfare: definitions, critiques, the information domain as the "invisible front", and Russian disinformation against Western Europe

### Takeaway
"Hybrid warfare" began as a US military concept about *battlefield* fusion of regular and irregular means (Hoffman 2007). After Crimea in 2014 it was stretched by NATO and the EU into "hybrid threats": coordinated, often non-military actions below the threshold of war that exploit democratic vulnerabilities and detection and attribution thresholds (Hybrid CoE). Leading scholars (Renz 2016; Galeotti 2016/2018; Fridman 2018; Mumford & Carlucci 2023; Caliskan 2019) warn that the term is analytically vague, politicised, and wrongly attributed to a Russian "Gerasimov doctrine". The rigorous move for the presentation is to (1) state which definition is used (Hybrid CoE / EU "hybrid threats", or Wigell's "hybrid interference"), (2) place disinformation as *one* instrument within it, and (3) acknowledge the critiques explicitly. Wigell (2019) fits best: hybrid interference as a *wedge strategy* exploiting the openness of liberal democracies. This is exactly the logic of cloned German news brands.

### Cited Findings
**Origin of the concept**

### Inferences
- **How to use the term rigorously in the title:** add a definitional sentence such as: *"In this study, 'hybrid warfare' is used in the sense of the EU / Hybrid CoE 'hybrid threats' concept: coordinated activity below the threshold of war that targets democratic vulnerabilities and exploits the limits of detection and attribution. Information operations are treated as one instrument of it (Wigell 2019: hybrid interference). Following Renz (2016), Galeotti (2018) and Caliskan (2019), we do not assume a unified Russian 'hybrid doctrine' (the 'Gerasimov doctrine' myth)."*
- **"Invisible front" (görünmez cephe):** it is best justified through (a) Hybrid CoE's "thresholds of detection and attribution", (b) Mumford & Carlucci's *ambiguity*, and (c) Wigell's covert wedge strategy. The GDELT result, where coverage of the takedown was concentrated in US and English-language media and barely in the targeted German sphere, can be presented as *empirical evidence of invisibility*: the targeted public was the least exposed to news of the exposure. This is an interpretation that should be checked against German-language GDELT sources.
- **Overstretch caution:** do not call every disinformation article an act of "war". Monaghan (2015), Renz (2016) and Caliskan & Liégeois (2021) warn that war framing can securitise ordinary politics and inflate Russian capability. Allen et al. (2020), Bail et al. (2020), Eady et al. (2023) and Ferrara (2017) all suggest limited measurable effects. Present Doppelgänger as *high-effort, plausibly low-impact* unless reach data say otherwise.
- **Geographic mapping link:** Lanoszka's (2016) scope condition (hybrid tools work best with local levers) and Szostek's (2017) "linkage" concept give a theoretical reason to *expect* geographic variation in targeting across Western Europe, for example Germany's large Russian-speaking population and energy dependence. This is a hypothesis for the GDELT mapping, not a finding.
