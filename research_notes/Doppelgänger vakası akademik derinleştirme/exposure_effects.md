>
> **Analytical frame used throughout (four distinct levels — never conflate):**
> 1. **Infrastructure size** (domains, accounts, Pages, bots, ad spend) — what the operation *built/spent*.
> 2. **Reach / exposure** (who could have seen / did see content; impressions, visits) — requires audience data.
> 3. **Engagement** (likes, shares, comments, follows; authentic vs. inauthentic) — platform metrics, easily inflated by bots.
> 4. **Persuasion / behavioral effects** (attitude, belief, trust, vote change) — requires causal design.
> The presentation's indicators (311 spoofed domains; GDELT coverage of the takedown) sit at level 1 and at a fifth, separate level: **visibility of the counter-disinformation response in news media** — which is not exposure to the operation itself.

## Q1. What do major studies find on exposure to and effects of Russian IRA / foreign influence campaigns and untrustworthy/fake news websites?

### Takeaway
The best-identified US studies converge: exposure to Russian IRA content and to "fake news"/untrustworthy sites was real but small relative to overall news diets and extremely concentrated among a small, already-partisan (mostly conservative, older, highly politically engaged) minority; studies linking exposure to outcomes find no detectable effect on attitudes, polarization or voting. Evidence from Ukraine, where Russian state TV had large audiences, shows that effects *can* occur when reach is broad and sustained — so "low effect" is conditional on low/concentrated reach, not a general law.

**(b) Fake news / untrustworthy / imitation-style websites — exposure concentration**

- **Guess, A. M., Nyhan, B., & Reifler, J. (2020). Exposure to untrustworthy websites in the 2016 US election. *Nature Human Behaviour, 4*, 472–480. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0833-x** [V for title/findings; DOI/pages K]. Web-tracking panel linked to survey:

Using nationally representative TV/desktop/mobile panels, fake news ≈ 0.15% of Americans' daily media diet; news overall ≈ 14% of media time, dominated by TV. [K — figures from memory; verify.]
- **Guess, A., Nagler, J., & Tucker, J. (2019). - **Budak, C., Nyhan, B., Rothschild, D. M., Thorson, E., & Watts, D. J. (2024). Misunderstanding the harms of online misinformation. *Nature, 630*, 45–53. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07417-w** [K]. Perspective synthesising evidence that exposure to false/inflammatory content is low on average and concentrated in a narrow fringe of motivated users; warns against inferring harm from prevalence counts; calls for measuring exposure among fringe/high-risk groups and outside the US.
- **Altay, S., Berriche, M., & Acerbi, A. (2023). Misinformation on misinformation: Conceptual and methodological challenges. *Social Media + Society, 9*(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051221150412** [K]. Argues alarmist narratives overstate the prevalence and persuasive power of misinformation; directly relevant to calibrated framing.

**(c) Counter-evidence: where Russian information operations did have measurable effects**
- **Peisakhin, L., & Rozenas, A. (2018). Electoral effects of biased media: Russian television in Ukraine. *American Journal of Political Science, 62*(3), 535–550. https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12355** [K]. Natural experiment using terrain-driven variation in Russian TV signal reception in Ukraine; Russian TV exposure increased pro-Russian party vote share and pro-Russian attitudes (2014). Shows effects are plausible when reach is broad, sustained and via trusted mass channels — conditions *not* established for Doppelgänger.
- **Erlich, A., & Garner, C. (2023). Is pro-Kremlin disinformation effective? Evidence from Ukraine. *The International Journal of Press/Politics, 28*(1), 5–28. https://doi.org/10.1177/19401612211045221** [K]. Survey experiment in Ukraine: respondents had difficulty distinguishing pro-Kremlin disinformation from true statements; susceptibility varied by prior attitudes/language. Indicates belief-level vulnerability even if behavioral effects remain unknown.

### Inferences
- The literature supports a "concentrated, minority-exposure, minimal-detectable-persuasion" baseline for social-media-based foreign campaigns in high-choice Western media systems, but almost all well-identified evidence is US-centred (2016–2020) and Twitter/Facebook-centred. Extrapolating to German-language Doppelgänger audiences in 2022–2024 is an inference, not a finding.
- Null average effects do not rule out effects on small, already-receptive subgroups, agenda-setting/indirect effects (via domestic amplifiers or mainstream coverage), or effects on trust rather than vote choice. A study should say "we do not measure these" rather than "there were none."
- For the presentation: the 311-domain list and 95.8% Germany share describe *targeting intent and infrastructure investment*, not audience reach. The Guess/Grinberg/Eady designs show what would be needed to move from level 1 to levels 2–4.

### Gaps
- Exact Eady et al. exposure multipliers (news media vs. politicians vs. IRA) and sample size not confirmed from the article text this session.
- Bail et al. 2020, Allen et al. 2020, Guess-Nagler-Tucker 2019, Budak et al. 2024, Peisakhin & Rozenas 2018, Erlich & Garner 2023 details are [K]; resolve DOIs before final reference list.
- I found no peer-reviewed web-tracking or panel study measuring exposure to *Doppelgänger* sites specifically (German or otherwise) — a genuine gap the presentation can name as a research opportunity.

## Q2. What evidence exists on Doppelgänger's actual reach and engagement?

### Takeaway
Publicly reported evidence documents very large and persistent **infrastructure** (hundreds of spoofed domains, tens of thousands of bot accounts, ~1.8 million automated German-language X posts in one month) and moderate **paid promotion**, but small **authentic engagement** (Meta 2022: ~4,000 accounts following any of 703 Pages; <10 joining the Group). No public source reports independently measured **exposure** (unique human viewers) or **persuasion effects**. Leaked SDA documents indicate the operators measured success with self-reported output/engagement metrics that analysts consider inflated, and treated Western media coverage of the operation as a success indicator.

### Cited Findings

**Meta, 27 September 2022 takedown (first public Meta report on the network)**

- Audience/engagement: "About 4,000 accounts followed one or more of these Pages, less than 10 accounts joined this Group and about 1,500 accounts followed one or more of these Instagram accounts" — [Security Affairs quoting Meta](https://securityaffairs.com/136326/social-networks/meta-dismantled-russian-network.html) [V-sec; the user's recollection of "~4,000 followers" is confirmed, but note it is ~4,000 *Page* followers + ~1,500 *Instagram* followers, and Meta's follower counts can include inauthentic accounts]

### Inferences
- Across sources, Doppelgänger is a **high-infrastructure, high-persistence, low-authentic-engagement** operation. Where reach numbers exist (AI Forensics' ad reach), they derive from *paid* distribution measured in accounts reached, not attention or belief.
- Self-reported KPIs in SDA documents and bot-generated post counts are both vulnerable to inflation; the incentive structure ("client-facing perception hacking") means operator metrics should not be used as evidence of impact.
- The presentation's 95.8% Germany share is consistent with the German Foreign Office's focus and Meta's 2022 targeting description; it is evidence of *targeting priority*, not of German audience exposure.

### Gaps
- No public source found that reports **unique human visitors** to Doppelgänger spoofed domains (e.g., web-panel or Similarweb-type traffic) — would be needed for an exposure claim.
- The exact phrasing of Meta's 2022 and Q1-2023 statements on low authentic engagement could not be read in the primary text (about.fb.com blocked).
- French VIGINUM's June 2023 "RRN" report (reportedly ~355 typosquatted media domains) and the US DOJ's September 2024 seizure of 32 domains (affidavit quoting SDA internal goals) are known to me [K] but were not verified this session.
- SDA-claimed view/engagement totals in the leaks were not verified; avoid quoting specific numbers until checked in the Lund/MPF report or Foreign Affairs.

## Q3. "Perception hacking" and the effects of publicity about influence operations

### Takeaway
There is a theoretical (Gleicher/Meta; Rid) and growing experimental basis for the claim that publicity about influence operations can itself produce effects — inflating perceived threat, lowering trust in true news, and increasing support for speech restrictions — independent of the operation's actual reach. This is directly relevant to interpreting GDELT coverage of the Doppelgänger takedown: high media visibility of the *response* is not evidence of the *operation's* impact and may, under alarmist framing, generate its own downstream effects.

### Cited Findings
- Nathaniel Gleicher (Meta head of security policy), pre-2020 US election: "We see malicious actors attempt to play on our collective expectation of wide-spread interference to create the perception that they're more impactful than they in fact are"; a perception hack is "an attempt to weaponize uncertainty to sow distrust and division" — [NPR via WHYY, "A 'perception hack': When public reaction exceeds the actual hack" (Nov 2020)](https://whyy.org/npr-story/a-perception-hack-when-public-reaction-exceeds-the-actual-hack); [HPPR](https://www.hppr.org/2020-11-01/a-perception-hack-when-public-reaction-exceeds-the-actual-hack) [V]. Meta's 2021 report *Threat Report: The State of Influence Operations 2017–2020* also discusses perception hacking [K — not verified this session].
- Meta Q4 2022 Adversarial Threat Report: "client-facing perception hacking" by covert operators to impress paying clients — [Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/02/metas-adversarial-threat-report-q4-2022/) [V-sec]
- Rid (2024, Foreign Affairs): Western anxious coverage was Doppelgänger's "biggest boost" — [CIUS digest](https://ukrainian-studies.ca/?p=4323) [V-sec]
- **Jungherr, A., & Rauchfleisch, A. (2024). Negative downstream effects of alarmist disinformation discourse: Evidence from the United States. *Political Behavior, 46*(4), 2123–2143. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-024-09911-3** [V]. Survey experiment: alarmist framing of disinformation (T1) vs balanced account (T2). Only T1 produced negative downstream effects — increased support for heavily restrictive regulation of digital speech and lowered satisfaction with democracy; balanced framing lowered perceived threat; effects "rather small" — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11109-024-09911-3); [Academia Sinica talk](https://survey.sinica.edu.tw/?p=18474) [V]. (Title confirmed — the user's recollection is correct.)
- **Clayton, K., Blair, S., Busam, J. A., et al. (2020). Real solutions for fake news? Measuring the effectiveness of general warnings and fact-check tags in reducing belief in false stories on social media. *Political Behavior, 42*, 1073–1095. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-019-09533-0** [K]. General warnings about misleading content reduced belief in false headlines but also reduced belief in true headlines (spillover scepticism).
- **van der Meer, T. G. L. A., Hameleers, M., & Ohme, J. (2023). Can fighting misinformation have a negative spillover effect? How warnings for the threat of misinformation can decrease general news credibility. *Journalism Studies, 24*(6), 803–823. https://doi.org/10.1080/1461670X.2023.2187652** [K]. Misinformation-threat warnings reduced credibility of accurate news.
- **Hoes, E., Aitken, B., Zhang, J., Gackowski, T., & Wojcieszak, M. (2024). Prominent misinformation interventions reduce misperceptions but increase scepticism. *Nature Human Behaviour, 8*, 1545–1553. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01884-x** [K]. Partisan asymmetry in how citizens react to revelations of foreign interference — relevant to how publicity is received.
- Altay et al. (2023) and Budak et al. (2024) (see Q1) argue alarmism about misinformation can itself misdirect policy and trust [K].

### Inferences
- The presentation's GDELT findings measure the **visibility of the takedown/attribution discourse**. In light of perception-hacking theory and Jungherr & Rauchfleisch (2024), this visibility is a phenomenon with *potential effects of its own* (on threat perception, trust in media, regulatory preferences) and could even be the channel through which the operation achieved most of its audience. Framing recommendation: report coverage volume as "news salience of the counter-disinformation response," explicitly not as "impact of Doppelgänger."
- A balanced framing is also supported empirically: studies show balanced/precise communication reduces threat perception without the negative downstream effects — this justifies the study's deliberately non-alarmist stance.
- Avoid the opposite error: low engagement does not mean zero significance — the operation imposed costs (platform enforcement, sanctions, diplomatic action) and spoofed the brands of real outlets, which may have trust implications that are unmeasured.

### Gaps
- No study found that experimentally tests the effect of news coverage *about Doppelgänger specifically* (e.g., on German trust in Der Spiegel/Bild/FAZ whose brands were cloned).
- Evidence on publicity effects is mostly US-based; German/European replications not located this session.

## Q4. Research designs that could credibly measure exposure and effects for a case like Doppelgänger

### Takeaway
Credible next steps combine (1) audience-side measurement (consented web-tracking/browser panels linked to surveys) to estimate actual visits to the spoofed domains; (2) platform data via DSA Article 40 vetted-researcher access (delegated act adopted 2 July 2025; in force 29 Oct 2025) for impressions/reach/ad delivery; and (3) pre-registered survey experiments testing whether spoofed-brand articles are more credible/persuasive than identical content from unknown or authentic sources, plus experiments on how takedown coverage is framed.

### Cited Findings
- **DSA Article 40 data access:** Commission adopted the delegated act on researcher data access on 2 July 2025, creating the DSA Data Access Portal for researchers, VLOP/VLOSE data providers and Digital Services Coordinators; researchers must be vetted by a DSC and justify requests (Art. 40(8)) — [Law Society Gazette Ireland](https://www.lawsociety.ie/gazette/top-stories/2025/july/eu-act-to-allow-access-to-tech-firms-data/); [UKE Poland](https://uke.gov.pl/en/digitalservices/ews/the-european-commission-has-adopted-a-delegated-act-on-access-to-data-from-online-platforms-for-verified-researchers,3.html); [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/who-gets-to-see-inside-the-eus-new-rules-on-data-access-under-article-40-of-the-dsa); [Arcom](https://www.arcom.fr/en/procedures-approving-researchers-access-platform-data) [V]
- Delegated act entered into force 29 October 2025 — [UKE Poland](https://uke.gov.pl/en/newsroom/delegated-act-on-data-access-for-researchers-enters-into-force,423.html) [V-sec, single regulator source; check Official Journal]
- Public-data access under Art. 40(12) is outside the delegated act's scope (relevant for scraping/public-data research, which platforms must facilitate separately) — [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/who-gets-to-see-inside-the-eus-new-rules-on-data-access-under-article-40-of-the-dsa) [V-sec]
- Expert debate on researcher autonomy under the delegated act — [DSA Observatory (Nov 2024)](https://dsa-observatory.eu/2024/11/29/researcher-access-to-platform-data-experts-weigh-in-on-the-delegated-act/) [V for existence]
- **Web-tracking + survey linkage** is the method behind Guess, Nyhan & Reifler (2020) and Moore et al. (2023) — see Q1 [V/K]. Methodological guidance: **Stier, S., Breuer, J., Siegers, P., & Thorson, K. (2020). Integrating survey data and digital trace data: Key issues in developing an emerging field. *Social Science Computer Review, 38*(5), 503–516. https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439319843669** [K].
- **Account-linked panel designs** (survey respondents consenting to share social-media handles, matched against platform-released IO account lists) — Eady et al. (2023), Bail et al. (2020) [V/K]. For Doppelgänger, the analogue would match German panel respondents' X/Facebook follow networks or timelines against Meta/X-disclosed Doppelgänger accounts and the domain list.
- **Ad Library reach data**: AI Forensics' use of Meta Ad Library "reach" demonstrates a currently available (though coarse) exposure proxy for paid distribution — [AI Forensics](https://cmsbackend.aiforensics.org/uploads/No_Embargo_In_Sight_Press_Release_fa4eee5487.pdf) [V-sec]
- **Source-cue experiments:** **Dias, N., Pennycook, G., & Rand, D. G. (2020). Emphasizing publishers does not effectively reduce susceptibility to misinformation on social media. *Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review, 1*(1). https://doi.org/10.37016/mr-2020-001** [K] — publisher cues had little effect on accuracy judgments, implying a spoofed brand may matter less (or differently) than assumed; this is exactly the hypothesis a Doppelgänger experiment should test. Erlich & Garner (2023) offer a template for disinformation-discernment experiments with a Russian-content stimulus set [K].
- **Natural-experiment logic:** Peisakhin & Rozenas (2018) use exogenous variation in exposure; analogues for Doppelgänger could exploit takedown timing (domain seizures, Meta removal dates, X mass suspensions in Jan 2024) as interruptions in a time series [K; design inference].

### Inferences (proposed designs for the presentation's "future research" slide)
1. **Exposure estimation (level 2):** Match the 311-domain list (and later domain lists from VIGINUM/DOJ/Auswärtiges Amt) against a consented German web-tracking panel (e.g., commercial or academic browser-extension panels) for 2022–2024; report % of adults with ≥1 visit, share of news diet, and concentration (top 1%/top 20% share), mirroring Guess et al. (2020) and Grinberg et al. (2019) metrics for comparability.
2. **Platform reach (levels 2–3):** Apply under DSA Art. 40(4) for impressions, unique reach and authentic-vs-inauthentic engagement on Meta/X content linking to the domains; separately use Art. 40(12) public data and the Meta Ad Library for ad reach by country/age.
3. **Engagement authenticity (level 3):** Separate bot-to-bot interactions from human engagement (network-based coordinated-behavior detection), since government counts (50,000 accounts, 1.8 M posts) are output, not audience.
4. **Persuasion/credibility (level 4):** Pre-registered 2×3 survey experiment in Germany: identical pro-Kremlin article presented as (a) spoofed Spiegel/Bild clone, (b) unknown outlet, (c) authentic outlet with correction; outcomes: perceived credibility, belief, policy attitudes (sanctions, aid to Ukraine, refugees), and trust in the cloned brand after debunk exposure.
5. **Publicity effects:** Experiment varying framing of takedown coverage (alarmist vs. calibrated, following Jungherr & Rauchfleisch 2024) on trust in news, perceived threat, support for regulation — links directly to the presentation's GDELT component.
6. **Interrupted time series:** Use takedown dates (Sep 2022 Meta; Jan 2024 X suspensions; Sep 2024 DOJ seizures) to test whether domain re-registration and coverage spikes correlate, treating media salience as an outcome, not a proxy for effect.

### Gaps
- Could not verify whether any Doppelgänger-specific Art. 40 data request or approved project exists yet.
- No located German web-tracking study reporting visits to Doppelgänger domains; feasibility depends on panel size, since Grinberg/Guess-style concentration implies a very small number of visitors in typical panels (statistical power concern — a design limitation to flag).
- Ethics/legal constraints (GDPR for tracking data) not researched in this session.
